02 · The argument

The prompt is the smallest part.

It's 11pm. Someone posted a prompt that promises to 10x your productivity. You save it. You'll never use it. The internet is selling you the prompt because the prompt is the part you can screenshot.

Meanwhile, at StubHub, a prompt is quietly running. No clever framework. Not a one-liner. A system. Wait times collapsed from 20+ minutes to near-instant. Support costs dropped 30%+.

The prompt
20%
What gets screenshotted. What gets posted. What gets sold.
The system
80%
What makes it actually work. What nobody is writing about. What this book is.

Structured output is the contract.

Every prompt returns an object. JSON with named fields. Not prose. Without this you don't have a system. You have a chatbot.

Negative instructions do the work.

The model's defaults are mediocre because the internet is mediocre. The "do not" list keeps those defaults out of your output.

Failure modes are the spec.

The What Breaks section on every page isn't a warning label. It's the specification. A prompt without documented failures isn't finished. It's a draft.

Observability is the difference.

Confidence scores. Reasoning fields. Signals used. Not for the human reader — for the engineering team that has to read why when it's wrong.

"The prompt is the artifact. The system around it is the work. This book is the diagram."

03 · What's in here

28 prompts. 5 stories. One frame.

Each prompt: the text, the system it powers, the source, the failure modes the team caught in production. Between every section is a story about the person running the prompt — and what happens when it goes wrong.

From Scene Four · The message that ended a contract

The message went out at 11:47pm on a Sunday. It was an auto-reply, drafted by a prompt the founder had built two weeks earlier. The system worked beautifully on the test set.

On Sunday night, when the founder was asleep, the prompt received an email from his largest customer's CTO and classified it as "out_of_scope: sales pitch."

The CTO read the auto-reply at 6:14am Monday. The CTO forwarded it to his board. The CTO ended the contract at 11am.

The prompt
Classifies incoming support tickets by priority (P1–P4) and routes them to the right Slack channel without a human in the triage loop.
SystemYou are a customer support triage specialist. You receive incoming support tickets and classify them by priority. Read the ticket subject, body, customer plan, and any context provided. Assign exactly one priority level: - P1 (Critical): Service is down, data loss, security incident... [truncated · full prompt on page 10]
What breaks
  • Free-tier users reporting genuine outages get under-classified as P3.
  • Confidence below 0.7 should route to a human queue. The router happily fires anyway.
  • Long ticket bodies cause the model to anchor on the last paragraph and miss the actual incident.
  • Frustrated tone leaks into priority. Tone is not severity.
